# Every Resources guard lives in the Resources plane

**Status:** Pass 2 — phases written against a design intent settled across three review passes. **Stage 2 re-runs next**, on this file as conformed on 2026-09-25 to what [mesh-entry-and-walk-gaps.md](archive/mesh-entry-and-walk-gaps.md) shipped under it; that pass reopened two questions, now D20 and D21. Scoped to the **Galaxy and the Star**, the two hosts that compose the plane. **The Resources leg of the security-legibility work**, after `docs/vision/auth.md`, [ADR-015](../docs/adr/015-passage-and-dominion.md)'s passage/dominion vocabulary, and the Registry routing rewrite that put its guards in one readable table. Judge this as a change to how the access-control model is reasoned about, not as a refactor.

**Objective — answering *"who is allowed to do this to a resource?"* takes reading one bounded region of code.**

**Why: anything the Star and the Galaxy each do for themselves can come to differ, and every difference is a place for complexity, a bug, or a hole to live** (Larry, 2026-09-25). Guards lead because a difference costs most there, but anything the two hosts do separately is in scope for the same reason. The ontology-version check is not a guard, and its two copies had drifted into two different bugs (D20).

**Three goals, in the order they matter:**

1. **The answer stops being split across the host and the plane.** It is in seven places today: `requirePassage` in `onBeforeCall`, the `requireDominionHere` decorators, the identity derivations inside host method bodies, the permission calls inside `Resources`, those inside `Subscriptions`, none in `QuerySubs` (which filters at delivery instead), and the `OrgTree` sites including the dominion short-circuit. Seven places is not an audit, it is an archaeology.
2. **No guard sits where a reader would not think to look.** Those derivations are not plumbing. `clientId` decides whose subscriber row is written and whose row a delete removes, and the code says so: *"`clientId` is supplied by the Handler-1 wrapper from `callChain[0]` (NEVER a param), so a client can only drop its OWN row."* That is a guard, it lives on the host, and it is copied per host — `unsubscribe` matches statement-for-statement across `star.ts` and `galaxy.ts`.
3. **Every name answers "what is this?" without being asked, starting with the plane itself — it is the RESOURCES plane, never the "data plane"** (Larry, 2026-08-23: *"what we will forever more call the resources plane"*). The temporal storage engine is a thing called `Resources` *inside* a thing called `ResourceDataPlane`, and `QuerySubs` opens by calling itself *"a near-clone of `Subscriptions`"*. Names you have to ask about are a model you cannot hold.

⚠️ **Every count in this file is a measurement, not a fact — the phase re-runs each one with an ANCHORED pattern.** An unanchored grep counts the JSDoc prose that discusses a decorator alongside the decorator itself, and both hosts discuss theirs at length; the dominion tally was written wrong twice for exactly that reason before anyone anchored it. The instruments: `grep -rcE '^\s*@mesh\(requireDominionHere' apps/nebula/src/*.ts | awk -F: '{s+=$NF} END{print s}'` and `grep -cE 'callContext\.callChain' apps/nebula/src/star.ts apps/nebula/src/galaxy.ts` (re-measured 2026-09-25: **5**, and **14 + 10**), and `grep -cE '^\s*@mesh\(' <host>` (**21** Galaxy, **20** Star).

The sections below give the guard model these goals imply, the surface it allocates, and the phases that build it.

## Context and current state

**Built already, and what becomes of each part:**

- **`ResourceDataPlane`** — one class, composed by a host with a single `new`, holding five sub-capabilities. **Adapted**: it becomes the plane itself and takes the name, so a host reads `#resources = new Resources(...)`.
- **`Resources`** (683 LOC) — the **temporal storage engine**: Snodgrass snapshot sequences over the host's SQLite, calling `OrgTree.requirePermission` at five sites. **Adapted**: keeps its job, and becomes `Snapshots` (D18) so the plane can take the name.
- **`OrgTree`** (528 LOC) — nodes, edges, grants, resolution, and the `hasDominionOver` short-circuit. **Carried over unchanged**, and it is the model the rest should match: its guards are already in the methods they protect.
- **The per-host data-plane entries** — bare `@mesh()` methods that derive `clientId`/`subscriberBinding`, fail closed, gate the client's pinned ontology version where they take one, then dispatch. **Left behind**: the derivation moves into the door, and D20 moves the version check in with it. Of the Galaxy's 21 decorators and the Star's 20, eight per host are the resource entries and one more is `invite`.
  - **Nothing here is dead, so this is a regrouping and not a deletion.** Every entry still has callers outside its own file; `subscribeQuerySubscribers` has 19. A phase that expects to delete rather than move will find nothing to delete.
- **The host capabilities** — `resetDevData` on the Star, and the three config pairs (D11). **Carried over unchanged**; they are not Resources operations. `setOntology` is not among them: it carries no decorator at all, and its JSDoc makes that absence the not-remotely-callable boundary, so adding `requireDominionHere` would open a door rather than close one. Only the config setters carry dominion; `getStarConfig` and `getGalaxyConfig` are bare `@mesh()`.
- **The Galaxy's four source entries** — `readSource`, `writeSource`, `buildNow`, `applyOntology` — keep their own `@mesh(requireChatWrite)` rather than collapsing behind the door. They look consumer-less, and that reading is wrong: `LOOP_TOOL_ENTRIES` maps each codegen tool to the entries it may reach, so those decorators **are** the model's authority model, and a test reads the table to assert every named entry sits at the chat floor. A door would hide a mapping a reader currently sees at a glance.
  - **`applyOntology`'s absence from that table is already structural.** `LoopEntry` is a closed union of the three source entries and the table is `as const satisfies`, so naming `applyOntology` in it does not compile (TS2322, reproduced). The obligation is to keep `LoopEntry` closed, not to add a mutation test for a hole the compiler already shuts.
- **`getCurrentOntology` and `getOntologyVersion` are CARRIED OVER UNCHANGED, and both stay BARE.** Their access control is upward passage — the Star's lazy pull of `getCurrentOntology` rides the asking member's own claims, which every member has — so adding a guard because the neighbours have one would break the pull for ordinary members (`calibration.md` §14). A subscription was weighed and rejected: a Star converges on current only when a tab asks for a version it lacks, so a Star nobody is using costs nothing, where a push would fan every Apply out to every Star. ⚠️ **Renaming them belongs to [nebula-ontology-history-file.md](nebula-ontology-history-file.md)**, which reserves the Star-fetch successor — the path both methods serve — as its own decision (§ *Tabled here, decided here: compiled-validator storage*). Its § *The scheme* lists `getOntologyVersion` among the methods that die, and names `getLatestOntologyVersion`, which `getCurrentOntology` has since replaced. They are not Resources operations, and a rename here would land on methods that file deletes or reserves.

- **`invite`, the reapers, and the eviction drains** — host code acting on a *third party's* rows, which is what § *The surface, allocated* exists to place.

### The registries are one kind

**`Subscriptions` (263) · `QuerySubs` (204) · `QuerySubscriberListSubs` (91) · `TreeSubscriptions` (69) merge into one, which keeps the name `Subscriptions` (D18).** All four carry the identical triple `clientId · subscriberBinding · subscribedAt` — the delivery address — and differ on two orthogonal axes: an optional TOPIC column, and an optional AUTHORIZATION identity.

| Registry | PRIMARY KEY | Topic | Authz identity |
|---|---|---|---|
| `Subscribers` | `(resourceId, clientId)` | `resourceId` | `sub` + dominion bit |
| `QuerySubscribers` | `(queryHash, clientId)` | `queryHash` | `sub` + `profileId` + dominion bit |
| `QuerySubscriberListSubs` | `(queryHash, clientId)` | `queryHash` | none |
| `TreeSubscribers` | `(clientId)` | — | none |

**Every key is `(topic, clientId)` with the topic degenerate where there is none, and two kinds share a topic.** A chat participant is a data subscriber AND a roster watcher of the same query, so both rows carry one `queryHash` — the dual-role shape `subscriber-list.test.ts` pins. That is the merge: one table keyed `(kind, topic, clientId)`, `sub` and the dominion bit nullable, and the tree kind's topic pinned to `''`, since a NULL there either fails the insert or lets one client hold two tree rows. **The line that exists in the data is AUTHORIZATION, not resource-ness** — a row with a `sub` gets a per-push permission evaluation and a row without does not — and the merged table says so where the nullable column is declared, because that is the only place a reader learns why it is nullable. `QuerySubscriberListSubs` opens by calling itself *a near-clone of `TreeSubscriptions`*, which is the same fact stated from the inside.

- **`profileId` becomes REQUIRED on rows that carry a `sub`.** Its optionality exists for a pre-rollout TOKEN — `SubscriberEntry`'s JSDoc states it as prose — and what closes it is not the wipe but [ADR-013](../docs/adr/013-identity-profileid-resolution.md): every post-wipe mint carries the claim. The chat fallback render subscribes to the profile this id names, so an absent id is a render hole rather than a valid state. It stays absent on the kinds that carry no `sub` — there is no person on those rows to name.
- ⚠️ **The merge silently widens what the drains touch, and the phase owes a decision there.** `clearSubscribers`, `clearQuerySubscribers` and `clearWatchers` are three calls on three tables today, so the Star's ontology install wipes resource and query subscriptions and leaves the org-tree ones alone. Against one table, "clear" has to mean *clear by kind* or an install starts dropping tree subscribers for a reason that has nothing to do with them.
- **`subscribeTree` belongs on every host's `requests`, because the orgTree is the PLANE's, not the host's** (D14). A host does not have an orgTree; its plane builds one, every time. The Galaxy's `onDagChanged` no-op reads like an absent capability and is not one — it is an unwired notification, which D15 deletes by moving the fan-out into the plane that already holds every input it needs: the merged registry has the watchers, `OrgTree` has `getState()`, and the bridge does the broadcast. ⚠️ **This is the error the `Dag` naming produced**, an hour before D16 was written: *"no org-tree subscribe channel on Galaxy"* plus a hook called `onDagChanged` read as *the Galaxy has no tree*, and a per-host surface was nearly designed on it. `Dag` names the data structure, so the question sounds structural; `OrgTree` names the concept, and "does this plane have an orgTree" answers itself.

### The reload channel is deleted

**The plane is not the mechanism for `.dev` Star reloads, and will not become one.** That is decided elsewhere, not inferred here: [nebula-scope-moves-to-subdomain.md](nebula-scope-moves-to-subdomain.md) § *Decisions* weighed **a subscription to the built digest** — naming this plane, and that it *"already carries enrollment and reaping"* — against a point-to-point nudge, and took the nudge, because a plane subscription *"reloads every teammate watching the galaxy the moment any build lands"* when the signal is wanted for whoever asked and nobody else. Its criteria pin both halves of the successor: only the client that asked for a build has its preview reloaded, and a change Studio makes to the dev Star reaches the dev tab **as a push, with no reload at all**.

**So the channel has no future role, and it has no present one either.** Its original trigger was the ontology-install push to Stars, and the pull replaced it — `nebula-client.ts` says so at the site: on a stale version `Star.read` throws `OntologyStaleError` and the reject path fires `onShouldRefreshUI`, *"relocated from the old push handler"*. `broadcastReload` has no production caller, so a row is written, never fired, and never reaped. ⚠️ Its JSDoc has meanwhile begun inventing a justification — it claims two hosts hold one and that `Galaxy.broadcastReload` fires on build completion, and the Galaxy has neither.

⚠️ **The sweep reaches the scaffold, which is why it is written out rather than left to the phase.** The subscriber is not test-only: `container/app/src/nebula.ts` wires `onReload: () => window.location.reload()` for every dev preview, and that file is frozen verbatim in `scaffold-seed.ts`, so **every generated app ships it**.

- **Source:** `reload-subscriptions.ts`; the Star's field, `subscribeReload`, `broadcastReload` and `onReloadBroadcastResult`; and `resetDevData`'s capture→wipe→restore **with its five-line JSDoc**, which exists only to preserve these rows.
- **Client:** the public `onReload` option, the subscribe-on-connect branch it gates, and `handleReload`.
- **Scaffold:** the `isDevPreview` block in `container/app/src/nebula.ts` **and its comment**, plus the frozen copy in `scaffold-seed.ts`. ⚠️ That comment is wrong today whatever happens here — it tells a user-developer that `onReload` re-syncs their dev preview, and `onShouldRefreshUI` is the only mechanism that fires.
- **Tests:** the baseline helpers `inspectReloadSubscribers`, `callStarInspectReloadSubscribers`, `callStarSubscribeReload` and `broadcastReloadForTest`, and the `reloadCount` capture. `reload-version-contract.test.ts` is **renamed and pruned, not deleted** — next paragraph.

⇒ **`resetDevData` does not survive this as "carried over unchanged."**

⚠️ **`reload-version-contract.test.ts` is mostly the SUCCESSOR's tests, filed under the dead mechanism's name — so it is renamed and pruned, never deleted.** Its header is stale and describes a connect-gate test that no longer exists; its `it()` titles are what it actually tests. It reads two counters for two mechanisms: `previewReadyCount` counts `handlePreviewReady`, the point-to-point build reply the subdomain file decided on, and `reloadCount` counts `handleReload`, the channel being deleted. **T3 — *the build reply goes to the REQUESTER ONLY* — is the one test that tells point-to-point from fan-out**, and reds if a fan-out is ever restored, so it enforces the very decision D17 rests on. Deleting the file would have deleted that enforcement.

## Design intent, constraints, and future state

**The contract, in three tiers.** A Resources operation passes exactly three access checks, each in one place — D20's version rule is not access control, and lives in the plane for the same reason. **The host's `onBeforeCall` requires passage and decides nothing else.** **The facade a gate returns supplies what the caller may not state** — the client's own id from `callChain[0]` on the request leg, a dead client's id from `callContext.callee` on the response leg. **The plane method below holds permission and ownership**, and is reachable from no wire at all. A forgeable parameter therefore survives on the plane harmlessly, because nothing outside can name the method that takes it.

**What the wire reaches, stated once, because every freeze and audit below counts against it.** A request-leg chain reaches a `@mesh()`-marked member anywhere on the host's prototype chain up to `LumenizeDO`, and whatever is reachable from what that member returns — through `requests`, that includes every public `OrgTree` method behind the `orgTree` getter. Nothing else, since [mesh-entry-and-walk-gaps.md](archive/mesh-entry-and-walk-gaps.md): op 0 of a wire chain must name a marked member, a `svc`-led chain is refused like any other unmarked one, and a reply is data, never a chain. So the response leg runs only continuations a node wrote, never one a client wrote.

**Two gates, one door.**

```ts
#resources = new Resources(...)   // the plane — the host calls it directly, never RETURNS it

@mesh() get resources()      { return this.#resources.requests }  // request leg
get resourcesResults()       { return this.#resources.results }   // response leg — no @mesh()
```

**The plane owns its two surfaces, so a host does one `new` and nothing else.** `requests` and `results` are named for the LEG, which is the one distinguisher true of every member. A host that assembled them itself could wire them wrong, forget one, or hand the wrong plane to one of them; this way the capability carries its own shape, which is ADR-007's idea applied a level down. ⚠️ **The host still calls the plane directly** for the members on neither surface — `ensureResource`, `findCurrentByField` and `streamProgress`. What it never does is *return* it. The three `clear*` drains stay inside, because the install that calls them is the plane's own (D20).

**`resourcesResults` is not a second door.** `ocan/execute.ts` checks a chain's entry op against the member-level check, so an unmarked getter is refused there exactly as an unmarked method is — and it is refused by reading the property descriptor, so an unmarked getter never runs. `__handleResponse` runs the same path at `requireMeshDecorator: false`, so a fire-back does not consult the mark while `onBeforeCall` still requires passage. A gate with no decorator is therefore response-open and request-closed, and confers that on everything behind it.

**"Response leg" means two different paths, and only one of them runs `onBeforeCall`.** Both are request-closed, so the security conclusion holds for each; what differs is how a `results` member is reached.

- **A reaper runs LOCALLY on the host that broadcast.** `svc.broadcast`'s flat branch — the one pinned ON — makes a 4-arg call to a Gateway, which returns `ClientDisconnectedError` as its ack rather than early-acking, and `lmz-api.ts` then runs the handler in place: `executeFilledChain(filled, nodeInstance, { requireMeshDecorator: false })`. No hop, no `__handleResponse`, so **tier 1 of the contract does not apply on this path at all** — nothing arrives from outside to be admitted.
  - ⚠️ **The `result` it receives can be the subscriber's own reply**, relayed by the Gateway. So `result` decides only WHETHER to reap. WHO is `callContext.callee`, which the framework stamps from the address the push used, so a forged error reaps only its forger.
- **`onInviteResult` genuinely arrives through `__handleResponse`**, as a fire-back from the facade, a different node. That path runs `executeEnvelope` at `requireMeshDecorator: false`, and `onBeforeCall` still requires passage.
- **The tier branch lands on that second path too.** `__forwardBroadcastResult` fires the origin's `__handleResponse` rather than a request, so an unmarked `results` gate receives it like any fire-back, filled by the same `replaceNestedOperationMarkers` the local path uses. It is pinned off, and on it a reaper reads the RECEIVING node's own name as the callee and deletes nothing — the `directThreshold` row in [backlog.md](backlog.md) § *Lumenize Mesh* owns that.

**The two gates are told apart by the LEG.** Not by where the caller id comes from: `onInviteResult` takes an `InviteSummary` rather than a dead client's id, and `read` needs no caller id at all. The leg holds for every member; identity holds for most.

⚠️ **Two absences carry the design, and both are invisible at the site that depends on them.** Adding `@mesh()` to `resourcesResults` — the tidying reflex, since every other gate has one — opens every continuation target to any logged-in browser in one edit, `onInviteResult` included. Spelling a surface's plane reference `private` rather than `#` puts every plane method back on the wire behind the door (D13). **Each owes an assertion that can see an absence**, and `getMeshGuard` cannot: `mesh-decorator.ts` sets `MESH_GUARD` only when a guard argument is passed, so it reads falsy for a bare `@mesh()` and an undecorated member alike. Read the mark off the DESCRIPTOR, the same lookup the entry rule makes: `isMeshCallable(Object.getOwnPropertyDescriptor(Galaxy.prototype, 'resourcesResults')?.get)` is `false`. Reading `Galaxy.prototype.resourcesResults` instead INVOKES the getter with the prototype as `this`, where the `#resources` brand check throws. Then assert each surface exposes no member beyond its row in the table.

**Seen from the client, this collapses many ways into one.** Today `nebula-client.ts` reaches the host through a distinct continuation per entry — `ctn<Star>().transaction(...)`, `.subscribe(...)`, `.subscribeQuery(...)`, and the rest — plus the chained `orgTree()` gate. Afterwards every one is `ctn<Host>().resources.<op>(...)`: one way to reach the plane from every caller, and a new client method cannot invent a second. `OrgTree` is reached as `resources.orgTree.setPermission(...)` — **no parens on `orgTree`, it is a getter** — and `ocan/execute.ts` refuses a chained `apply` on it as not a function. The sweep is every call through today's tree gate: `nebula-client.ts`'s `orgTree` mutators, the baseline test-app's tree wrappers, and the `mesh-entry-reach` scenario, which walks past the gate on purpose. Larry: *"If we have two, why not three? why not 10?"* A second door is not a smaller version of the mess; it is the first instance of it.

**The surface, allocated.** A gate is what makes `public` on the plane mean CALLABLE FROM THE WIRE. Nothing returns the plane today — the only gate returns the tree — so `public` there costs nothing and no method was ever chosen for a wire audience. Neither gate returns the plane; each returns a surface whose member list is the whole of what it exposes.

| Facade / plane | Member → the plane op it wraps |
|---|---|

**The `do*` prefix goes with the PARAMETERS, not with the method.** The plane's ops keep their `clientId` argument; the door derives it and passes it down. That is the whole reason the plane can hold a forgeable-looking parameter safely — and it is the tension the two surfaces dissolve rather than pay. `removeSubscriber(resourceId, clientId)` has two callers of opposite provenance: `unsubscribe`, where a client drops its OWN row and the id is derived, and the reaper, where the id belongs to a client already gone. Self-guarding serves neither — derive, and the reaper cannot name the dead; accept, and the parameter is forgeable. Keeping the raw op on an unreachable plane serves both.

**Why not the two cheaper shapes.** Self-guarding the members on neither surface fails on most of them: `ensureResource`'s server-composed `actor` is the point of the method, `findCurrentByField` takes no node argument to check, and the three `clear*` take no arguments at all, so there is nothing to derive and a wire caller would drop every subscriber on the host. Pulling their callers inside fails on the layer map — the Galaxy's agent-message commit is host business logic, not a resource capability.

**`streamProgress` is a request-leg PUSH, and D19 moves it into the plane's host-only set.** It is `protected` on `galaxy.ts` today, building the chat query inline and reaching `targetsForQuery` through the Galaxy's own `queryTargets` seam before `this.broadcast`. It takes no `result`, and its `progress` and `replyTo` are caller-supplied, so behind a response-open gate any passage-holder could inject arbitrary text into a shared chat attributed to someone else's live turn — `replyTo`'s JSDoc makes that attribution load-bearing against a hang. Because the plane has no `this.lmz`, the push rides a `ResourceHostBridge` member of its own, exactly as `fireInvite` does (Phase 4).

**It moves with query/resource parameter names, so the chat vocabulary stays on the Galaxy.** Today's body builds `{ queryType: 'parentChild', typeName: 'Message', field: 'chat', value: chatId }` itself. Moved verbatim, that puts the chat ontology into the plane every host composes — the layering § *Why not the two cheaper shapes* rejects. The plane's `streamProgress` takes the query; the Galaxy's codegen loop builds it.

### One version rule (D20)

**A host serves one ontology version, its source's current one, and the plane enforces that the same way on every host.**

1. A client pinned to the installed version proceeds.
2. Otherwise the plane asks its source for the current row, and installs it if it differs from what is installed. The pinned client proceeds if it named that row — at once when the source answers at once, or on its retry after `installing` when the source answers by fire-back.
3. Any other pinned version is answered `OntologyStaleError`, and the client refreshes onto the current bundle.

A host with nothing installed is case 2, so the Star's first-touch pull in `invite` and the Galaxy's self-seed are the same code as every other install.

**Each host keeps only its source (D3).** The Galaxy's chat ontology ships in the Worker bundle (`CHAT_ONTOLOGY_SEED_ROW`), so its source answers at once. The Star's app ontology lives in its Galaxy's registry, so its source is a `getCurrentOntology()` call answered by fire-back, and `onOntologyPulled`, where that fire-back lands, becomes a `results` member (Phase 7). The check, the install, the clear-by-kind drains, and the three ways an operation answers a mismatch — `transaction` returns it, `read` throws it, `subscribe` pushes it — are one piece of code in the plane. ⚠️ **One step of the install stays host-owned:** a `wipeOnInstall` row wipes through the Star's `resetDevData`, which is `.dev`-guarded and wipes the whole DO, so the plane's install reaches it through a `ResourceHostBridge` member, as `fireInvite` does.

**Two copies of this check drifted into two bugs — this task's case in one example.** Both hosts stored one installed version and compared the pinned version against it. They differed only on a mismatch, and each side's answer was wrong:
- **The Star installed whatever version a client pinned, an older one included.** A tab still on v1 after v2 was applied — a collaborator's preview, say, since the build reply reloads only the requester's — made the Star pull and install v1. `#installState` deleted v2's row and dropped every subscriber, while `setOntology` left v2 as the last entry of the history it reads the installed version from. While warm, v2 clients were validated against v1; after eviction every operation threw *index/row drift*; a `wipeOnInstall` v2 re-wiped the `.dev` Star on each flip back. ⇒ An install must record its row as THE installed one in the same write — the drift was a row installed while the record still named another.
- **The Galaxy never installed after first touch.** `#ensureChatFacet` seeds only an empty Galaxy, and Studio pins `CHAT_MESSAGE_ONTOLOGY_VERSION` from its own bundle, so the first bump of that label — still `chat-message-v1` — would answer every existing Galaxy's Studio with `OntologyStaleError` indefinitely. Until Phase 6 lands, bumping it needs a wipe; after it, a breaking chat change needs the migration story the Star's `wipeOnInstall` stands in for.

**The Star half is fixed ahead of this task, proven red first** (2026-09-25), because two collaborator tabs on one `.dev` Star were enough to trigger it.
- **Red on the old code, both limbs:** in the `star-serves-current-ontology` scenario, a stale tab's request cost a current tab its subscription and made a v2-only type fail validation, and a revert was never served.
- **Each limb reds alone** under the mutation that isolates it — pulling the pinned version, or leaving a re-installed version where it was in the history.
- **The fix is this rule, written in the Star:** its pull asks for the Galaxy's current row, and `setOntology` moves a re-installed version to the end of the history every reader takes it from. `dev-star-data-lifecycle.test.ts` pins the revert half where CI runs it. Phase 6 moves the rule into the plane.
- **`Galaxy.getOntologyVersion` lost its last production caller** with it; [nebula-ontology-history-file.md](nebula-ontology-history-file.md) already lists it among the methods that die.

## The push path — what it stores, and what it rechecks

**Reading a permission costs no storage.** `OrgTree` keeps the tree as a lazily-built state cache with an adjacency index over it, invalidated on every mutation, and `resolvePermission` walks that index in memory. One `evaluatePermissions` call is a map walk, not a read. So the cost objection against checking at push time is weaker than it looks, and the design question is which shape stores the least, not which runs least often.

**The stored `dominionOverHostAtSubscribe` bit exists because the push path has no token.** A Galaxy or Universe admin holds no grant in the host's tree — they reach it by dominion, which is a fact about their claims. Nobody is calling when a push goes out, so a bare grant evaluation would drop exactly those people. The bit is that remembered claim, and something like it is unavoidable while pushes are authorized at all.

⚠️ **What is wrong is not that the bit is stored, it is what the recheck's own comment implies.** `evaluatePermissions` takes the bit as a short-circuit, so for a dominion-holding subscriber the tree is never consulted and the verdict is the one frozen at subscribe. The recheck is live for grant holders and a replay for everyone else, while the comment presents it as uniformly live. By ADR-010's replication rule the bit is a copy of a mutable fact and owes a stated convergence mechanism; it has one — the client re-subscribes on reconnect, re-deriving the bit from the fresh token — but that is written only in `NebulaClient`'s resubscribe walk, on the other side of the wire from the column it converges. **State both at the column.**

**Keep the per-push recheck. It stores the least of the available shapes.** For grants it evaluates live and remembers nothing, so there is no second verdict to go stale. For queries it is not a freshness check at all — it computes the allowed and denied sets per subscriber, which is what lets a denied subscriber be told *which* nodes they lost rather than silently dropped ([ADR-008](../docs/adr/008-full-org-tree-visibility.md)) — so it cannot move regardless. And the obvious alternative is worse: skipping the per-commit evaluation means materializing an allowed flag per row for the commit path to read, a second stored verdict beside the bit, and [ADR-013](../docs/adr/013-identity-profileid-resolution.md) calls a stored authorization verdict a security bug.

**There is no revocation window on the single-resource path.** A revoked grant is caught by the very next push, because `#broadcast` runs from the commit and evaluates live. Nothing is delivered in between, so nothing escapes. Queries differ, and not because they are safer: a permission change also fires an immediate rerun of every live query, and that exists to *disclose* the loss promptly under ADR-008, not to close a hole. ⚠️ Do not "fix" the single-resource path by adding it to that rerun — it would re-deliver on every DAG change to gain nothing, and wanting the two paths to look alike is what this note exists to stop.

**`registerQuerySubscriber` keeps no register-time check, and the merged registry keeps none either.** Refusing at registration is what ADR-008 forbids: a subscriber must be able to learn the denied set, and a refused registration discloses nothing. So it is not an exception to *every method guards itself* — its guard is authentication plus the delivery-time evaluation, and **that is what the method must say.** Recording it as *"always succeeds — no permission check"* is what made it read as a hole for two reviews running.
